    note: "The most famous back in art history. You have seen this on book covers, album covers, probably a motivational poster — and then you look at it properly and it is still good, which is the annoying thing about Friedrich. The fog does the heavy lifting: the view feels enormous precisely because you cannot see most of it. A few rock islands poke through, the rest is withheld, and your imagination fills in more mountains than any painter could supply."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-monet-w1524"
    note: "Monet checked into the [Savoy Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy_Hotel) and painted the Thames from his balcony, on and off, for five years — mostly because London fog kept repainting the city for him faster than he could keep up. Here Charing Cross Bridge, the water, the smoke and the trains all collapse into horizontal bars of mauve and gold. It is the same problem he would later set himself with the lily pond: how do you paint something whose edges never hold still?"

What gets me is the control behind it — Yoshida supervised every step of carving and printing — all spent on making the picture look like the moment control quietly slips away. The fade is done with [bokashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokashi_(printing)), gradation wiped by hand into the printing block, for every single impression."

I have a soft spot for paintings where the air is the main character. Fog gives a painter the oldest permission slip there is: you get to leave most of the picture out, and the picture gets *better*.

A thousand years apart, the painters in this issue all cash in that same permission. Dong Yuan lets riverbanks condense out of wet ink. Turner makes the sun fight for it. Friedrich hides an entire mountain range and hands you one rock to stand on. Monet finds the fog so productive he paints it for five years from a hotel balcony, and Yoshida carves it into woodblocks, where vagueness has to be planned precisely.

Yoshida's pale sea uses the same hand-wiped gradation Hasui used for rain in [Japanese rain](/newsletter/0002-japanese-rain). Rain moves and fog waits — but on paper they are nearly the same trick, and I find that quietly wonderful.
