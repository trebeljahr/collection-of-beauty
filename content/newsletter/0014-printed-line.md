The whole series — Death coming for pope, emperor, ploughman, everyone — fits on woodblocks roughly the size of a thumbprint, cut by Hans Lützelburger, maybe the finest woodcutter who ever lived. Before photography, prints were how images travelled. A painting stayed in one chapel; an engraving could be in fifty cities by spring. So the printed line is where art history actually moved — and where young artists did their learning, copying sheets like these.

This issue follows the line through five hands. The Chronicle's skeletons show how much life a few black contours can hold. Schongauer's demons get colour-checked against market fish by young Michelangelo. Dürer hides a solvable puzzle inside an unsolvable mood, Holbein and his doomed woodcutter fit Death onto thumbnail-sized blocks, and Rembrandt closes the issue by demonstrating the printmaker's rarest skill: leaving things out.

All of it black ink on paper. Cheapest materials in art; some of the most durable magic.
