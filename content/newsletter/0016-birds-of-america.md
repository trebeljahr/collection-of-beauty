    note: "Audubon's rule for *[The Birds of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birds_of_America)* was beautifully unreasonable: every bird at life size. The book was printed on the largest paper available — 'double elephant' folio, about a metre tall — and a flamingo still doesn't fit. So the bird bows. The whole neck swings down toward the ground in one pink arc, and what should be an awkward compromise becomes one of the most elegant images of the nineteenth century. I love when a constraint does the designing. The page said no, and the flamingo said fine, watch this."
  - id: "audubon-birds-47-ruby-throated-humming-bird"
    note: "The opposite problem: hummingbirds at life size leaves a lot of page. Audubon's answer is to fill it with a whole society — ten ruby-throats hovering, perching and squabbling around a trumpet creeper, iridescent throats caught at different angles like sequins under a moving light. It is less a specimen plate than a small soap opera with wings. The Ruby-throat is the only hummingbird that breeds east of the Mississippi, so for most Americans of his day, this one page *was* hummingbirds."
  - id: "audubon-birds-211-great-blue-heron"
    note: "The heron folds itself into right angles — neck down, legs bent — to stay life-size on the sheet, looking for all the world like a bird doing limbo under the top edge of the page. The plate is built up in layers: [aquatint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatint) tones from the copper plate, then watercolour by hand, sheet after sheet. An army of colourists in London painted every impression of every plate this way. The folio took eleven years to finish. Nobody sensible would start such a project, which is presumably why it is the greatest bird book ever made."
  - id: "audubon-birds-351-great-cinereous-owl"
    note: "The great grey owl faces you dead-on, those huge concentric facial discs swirled out of aquatint like a fingerprint. Audubon never saw this bird alive — he worked from a skin shipped south by a correspondent — and you can feel the slight tilt toward myth: the head a touch grander than life. Which, to be fair, is true to the species anyway. A great grey owl is mostly feathers and attitude; the actual bird inside is startlingly small. The plate exaggerates honestly."
  - id: "audubon-birds-26-carolina-parrot"
    note: "Seven Carolina parakeets crowd a cocklebur branch in green, yellow and orange — North America's only native parrot, painted when flocks of hundreds still moved through the woods. Farmers shot them as pests, trappers caged them, the forests fell, and the last one died in a zoo in 1918. So this plate has become something Audubon never intended: the best record of a colour that no longer exists in American trees. It is a cheerful, lively page, which somehow makes it harder to look at."

Audubon's *Birds of America* is what happens when one man refuses a sensible compromise for eleven straight years. Life size or nothing — flamingo included.

That single stubborn rule generates everything in this issue. Big birds bend and bow to fit the page like dancers in a low doorway. Tiny birds multiply into flocks to fill it. An owl Audubon never met stares out at full mythic scale, and a branch full of parakeets keeps a vanished species' colours in print two centuries after the trees went quiet.

Haeckel, in [Microscopic ornament](/newsletter/0013-microscopic-ornament), scaled his creatures *up* to make us see them. Audubon refused to scale at all — and the refusal turned a bird catalogue into one of the great works of American art.
