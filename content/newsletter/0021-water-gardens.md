---

Most painters change subjects. Monet changed *one* subject, for fifty years, until it gave up everything it had: water that reflects.

So this issue is a time-lapse of a single obsession. At Argenteuil in 1872, the reflections live politely inside a normal river view. At Montgeron, they start taking over the canvas. By Giverny he is no longer finding water but manufacturing it — pond, bridge, lilies, all planted as future paintings — and the horizon quietly leaves the picture and never comes back. Even Venice, on his one visit, gets processed into reflections. And the late grass-and-lily canvases barely bother being *of* anything: surface, sky and depth all happen on the same square metre of paint.

Whistler, back in [Candlelight and night](/newsletter/0010-candlelight-and-night), dimmed his river to almost nothing. Monet went the other way and turned his up — more colour, more surface, more pond — until one garden held the whole sky. Fifty years staring at water. It was enough.
