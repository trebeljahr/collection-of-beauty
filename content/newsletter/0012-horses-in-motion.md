The restraint *is* the propaganda: real power, the picture argues, doesn't need to gallop. Every equestrian royal portrait for the next two centuries — Van Dyck, Velázquez, the lot — is descended from this canvas. Titian set the template in one quiet evening ride."

Horses, hounds, hunters, crocodile and hippo all crash together in one spiralling diagonal painted for a Bavarian duke's hunting lodge. Kuniyoshi made his name on warrior prints exactly like this, all diagonal energy and patterned water, and the crossing still reads at full speed nearly two centuries later. Horses were how painters did speed before film existed. For a few thousand years, if you wanted motion on a wall, you wanted a horse.

What delights me in this five is the gearbox. Uccello runs the charge through a perspective grid until it half-freezes into geometry. Titian drops to a deliberate imperial walk. Rubens throws the throttle open on a hunt starring a hippo he had never seen, Géricault rears his chasseur up into history's worst-timed glory, and Kuniyoshi sends two samurai through a river at full gallop across three sheets of paper.

The ferries in [Crossing the water](/newsletter/0005-crossing-the-water) got people across politely, for a fare. This issue's answer is simpler: point the horse at the river and go.
