    note: "Karasaki's pine is a single dark mass lashed by night rain. The subject comes from the Eight Views of Omi, a Japanese answer to the older Chinese Eight Views of Xiao and Xiang, where weather and time of day turn landscape into poetry. Hiroshige keeps the palette close to monochrome and lets the rain-lines do most of the drawing. The famous pine at Karasaki Shrine is not described leaf by leaf; it is understood as a shape strong enough to hold against the storm."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-hiroshige-atake-sous-une-averse-soudaine"
    note: "A summer shower breaks over Shin-Ohashi Bridge and Atake in 1857, near the end of Hiroshige's life. Figures lean into the downpour, a boatman crosses the Sumida below, and the far bank is reduced to a blue-grey strip. The print is from One Hundred Famous Views of Edo, but its fame travelled far beyond Edo: Van Gogh copied it in oil in Paris in 1887. The original still feels startling because the bridge is cut off at the edges, as if the rain has cropped the city faster than the eye can follow."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-tokyo-junidai-samidare-furu-sanno-by-kawase-hasui"
    note: "Hasui brings the rain into the shrine precinct as a vertical screen. In this 1919 print from Twelve Subjects of Tokyo, early summer rain falls on the steps of Hie, or Sanno, Shrine while small figures tuck themselves under umbrellas. The shin-hanga revival kept the older division of labour between designer, carver, printer and publisher, but its atmospheres feel modern: softer gradations, quieter colour, more ordinary weather. Hasui's Tokyo is not a set of famous monuments so much as a city met in passing."

    note: "The name returns: Shin-Ohashi Bridge, now a steel structure in 1926 rather than Hiroshige's wooden span. Hasui frames the girders close, turns the sky almost black, and lets slanting rain flatten the river and city into blue-grey bands. The sheet comes from Twenty Views of Tokyo, the series in which he repeatedly set modern infrastructure inside old woodblock weather. Seen after Hiroshige's sudden shower, it becomes a quiet argument that rain can carry continuity better than architecture can."
---

Rain in these prints is drawn, not merely described. It comes down in parallel cuts, diagonal screens, soaked planes of grey and green. Hiroshige uses it to turn roads, bridges and shrine pines into events of weather.

Hasui inherits that grammar in twentieth-century Tokyo. The city has steel bridges now, and the printing has softer bokashi gradations, but the rain still does the same work: it measures distance, hides detail, and makes a passing figure feel briefly held inside the whole scene.
