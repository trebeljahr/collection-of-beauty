Ruins are architecture's afterlife, and painters can't leave them alone — partly for the obvious melancholy, but mostly, I suspect, because a ruin is a building with the doors removed. Everything becomes a threshold.

The five here each stand somewhere different on that line between standing and fallen. Mantegna leans his saint on collapsed antiquity. Rubens lets cattle graze the palace of the Caesars and finds it peaceful rather than tragic. Turner's river politely rebuilds an abbey in reflection, Schinkel — a real architect — paints the one cathedral he could never build, and Friedrich distills the entire genre into a single object: an iron gate, half open, mist beyond.

The bridges in [Bridges and crossings](/newsletter/0005-bridges-and-crossings) got you across to the other side. Friedrich's gate makes no such promise. It just stands there, ajar, and lets you decide — which is, I think, what ruins have been asking of us all along.
