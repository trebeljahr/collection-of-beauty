And in the middle of all this jewel-hard precision, a baby crumpling a page. Six hundred years of theology in the frame, and the most convincing detail is the toddler about to wreck a manuscript."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-giorgione-pala-di-castelfranco"
    note: "Giorgione painted this altarpiece for a small-town chapel built in grief. It stands over the tomb of Matteo Costanzo, a soldier dead at twenty-three, and the armoured saint at the left keeps watch on his behalf. The Virgin sits unusually high on her throne, above a landscape of the painter's home country, in the format Italians called a [sacra conversazione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacra_conversazione): saints from different centuries calmly sharing one space. It has hung in the same town for five hundred years."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-cranach-madonna-under-the-fir-tree"
    note: "Every Italian Madonna gets an Italian backdrop: soft hills, cypresses, golden air. Cranach, court painter in Saxony, looked at that convention and set his Virgin under spindly northern firs instead, with a proper German castle on the crag behind her. Such a small decision, such a complete relocation. Suddenly the holy family is at home in his landscape, conifers and all. I have a soft spot for painters who bring the sacred home like this."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-parmigianino-madonna-and-child-with-angels-known-as-the-madonna-with-the-long-neck"
    note: "Known, fairly, as the *Madonna with the Long Neck*. Everything is stretched: neck, fingers, the Child pouring off her lap, the column behind rising so high it never gets its capital. None of it is anatomy and all of it is on purpose. Parmigianino was chasing an elegance past the natural, the move that defines [Mannerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mannerism). He died at thirty-seven with the panel still unfinished, which somehow fits. The painting wants more than bodies and buildings can give, and it just keeps reaching."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-bartolome-esteban-perez-murillo-008"
    note: "Joseph gets the spotlight for once: bent down, playing with the toddler Jesus and a small goldfinch, while Mary looks up from her spinning. The goldfinch was traditional code for the Passion to come (legend says the bird plucked a thorn from Christ's crown), but Murillo plays the symbol deadpan. Here it is simply a pet in a carpenter's house at the end of a workday. The holiest family in painting, doing what families do in the evening."

Painters returned to the Madonna the way musicians return to a standard. The theme is fixed, so everything interesting happens in the interpretation.

That is what this issue is for. Rogier plays it precise, with a page-grabbing baby for honesty. Giorgione plays it elegiac, over a young soldier's tomb. Cranach transposes it north into the fir trees. Parmigianino bends every note past what anatomy allows, and Murillo brings it all the way down to earth: a workshop floor, a spindle, a pet bird.

Same mother, same child, five centuries apart, and you could never confuse one of these panels for another.
