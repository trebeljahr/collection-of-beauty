The engraver, Robert Bénard, cut around 1,800 plates for it over thirty years, most of them copied from earlier masters rather than fresh dissection: knowledge being industrialized, plate by plate. The hand lies palm-up like an offering. In a way it is one — here is what you're made of, page 4,000-something, see also: FOOT."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-newton-williamblake"
    note: "Blake's Newton crouches at the bottom of the sea, magnificent and naked, reducing the universe to a compass and a diagram — and the picture is an *attack*. Blake hated what he called single vision, measurement that stops seeing; 'Art is the Tree of Life, Science is the Tree of Death', he wrote, never one for nuance. The delicious irony: he made his anti-measurement manifesto so geometrically beautiful that scientists adopted it. A giant bronze version now sits outside the British Library. Blake would be furious, which only improves it."

Vermeer painted almost no men alone; this one (with its twin, *The Astronomer*) may portray his Delft neighbour [Van Leeuwenhoek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek), the cloth merchant who ground lenses and discovered microbial life. Proof unavailable, but I want it to be true: the man who found a universe in a water drop, painted by the man who found one in a Delft room."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-joseph-wright-of-derby-the-alchemist"
    note: "An alchemist hunting the philosopher's stone kneels before his flask as it erupts with light — and the joke history played is that this actually happened: in 1669 Hennig Brand boiled down unspeakable quantities of urine chasing gold and instead discovered phosphorus, the first new element found since antiquity. Wright paints the moment as an annunciation, the glowing flask in place of the angel. Wrong theory, real miracle. Half of science history in one image, lit by its own discovery."

I love paintings of science more than almost any other genre, because they catch knowledge while it is still warm — before the textbook flattens it into fact, when it is still wonder, theatre, obsession and occasionally bad smells.

This issue has all of those. Vesalius walks a flayed man through the Veneto hills and changes anatomy forever. The *Encyclopédie* mass-produces the body in labelled plates. Blake files painting's most beautiful objection to the whole project, Vermeer catches a geographer at the exact moment of insight, and Wright's alchemist gets the punchline: hunting magic, finding chemistry, unable to tell the difference in the glow.

The air-pump demonstration in [Candlelight and night](/newsletter/0010-candlelight-and-night) put science on stage for a living-room audience. These five are the quieter scenes — the lab, the study, the page — where the actual work got done.
