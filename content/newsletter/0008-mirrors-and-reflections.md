I find it genuinely funny that the painting containing a princess, her court, a dog and the painter himself is upstaged by the blurriest object in the room. Put a mirror in a painting and you've smuggled in a second painting. Painters have been using the trick for centuries, and the variety of what they smuggle is wonderful.

Caravaggio uses the water-mirror to close a doomed loop. Rubens uses a hand mirror to show a face and a back at once — the cheapest special effect in art, still working four hundred years on. Velázquez hides the two most powerful people in Spain inside the smallest, blurriest mirror he could paint, and Utamaro and Ingres use glass for the most human thing of all: checking the back of your own head.

The letters in [Books and reading](/newsletter/0004-books-and-reading) pulled a room's attention inward onto paper. Mirrors do the opposite trick — they fold the room back out at you, and sometimes they catch you standing there, looking.
