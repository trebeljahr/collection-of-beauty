Chen Rong painted it in 1244 and signed it with a poem claiming he worked with ink rinsed from his own hair, which I choose to believe. East Asian dragons are rain spirits, not villains — these aren't monsters to slay but forces to live alongside. The scroll is too long to ever display in full. Even in a museum, you only get part of the storm."

    note: "My favourite detail in all of dragon-slaying art: look at the princess. She isn't cowering — she has the dragon on a *leash*. The legend does say she leads the tamed beast to town, but most painters skip that part; Uccello paints the exact moment where the story is both a rescue and a dog walk. Saint George's storm cloud spirals behind him like it was drawn with a compass, the cave mouth is geometric, the dragon's wings carry neat target-mark roundels. Late Uccello is half fairy tale, half diagram, and entirely wonderful."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-hieronymus-bosch-triptych-of-temptation-of-st-anthony-wga2585"
    note: "Bosch's approach to monsters is volume. Saint Anthony kneels at the centre of the panel while around him an entire economy of hybrids gets on with its blasphemous business — fish that are also boats, jugs with legs, a burning village over there for atmosphere. The saint is almost lost in his own painting, which is of course the point: temptation isn't one big devil, it's ten thousand small distractions. Sixteenth-century collectors couldn't get enough; copies multiplied across Europe immediately. Bosch was that rare thing, a completely unexplainable artist with mass-market appeal."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-takiyasha-the-witch-and-the-skeleton-spectre"
    note: "The greatest reveal in Japanese printmaking: the sorceress Takiyasha reads her spell from a scroll, and the thing she has summoned through the ruined palace blinds is a skeleton the size of the building. Kuniyoshi runs it across all three sheets of the triptych and — this is the brilliant part — draws the skeleton with real anatomical accuracy, probably cribbed from imported Dutch medical books. Folklore monster, rendered with textbook bones. It is the single most metal image in the collection and I will not be taking questions."
  - id: "collection-of-beauty-johann-heinrich-fussli-059"
    note: "Fuseli built a career on painting nightmares — his most famous picture is literally called *The Nightmare* — and this one runs on pure bad timing: a giant serpent has already coiled around horse and rider before either understood what was happening. Horse and snake twist along the same diagonal, so tightly wound together that the eye can't fully untangle them, with one hard theatrical spotlight on the panic. Where Uccello's dragon ends up on a leash, Fuseli's serpent arrives mid-catastrophe. The Romantics didn't want monsters defeated. They wanted them *felt*."

Every culture invents monsters, and what it invents tells on it wonderfully. That's the real fun of this issue: five monsters, five completely different ideas of what a monster is *for*.

Chen Rong's dragons are weather — powers you coexist with, painted half-dissolved in their own ink. Uccello's dragon is a problem civilization solves, with lance and leash and geometry. Bosch's monsters are temptations, numberless and busy. Kuniyoshi's skeleton is a special effect with perfect anatomy, and Fuseli's serpent is the Romantic nightmare: the monster as something that happens to you too fast.

The demons in [Printed line](/newsletter/0014-printed-line) mobbed Saint Anthony in engraved black and white. Here the monsters get ink, oil, colour woodblock and a spotlight. They've done well for themselves.
